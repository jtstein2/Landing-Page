Every office has a spreadsheet named FINAL_v7

AI can now build the small, custom tools your business has been duct-taping together for years. Here's what that actually looks like.

November 17, 2026 · 2 min read

Somewhere in your company there is a spreadsheet called something like Inventory_FINAL_v7_USE_THIS_ONE.xlsx. It has fourteen tabs, three of them hidden, and one cell that turns red for reasons no living employee can explain. Everyone is afraid of it. Everyone depends on it.

For years, that spreadsheet was the best option going. Custom software was for companies with an IT department and a budget line that literally said “custom software.” Everyone else got a subscription to something built for a business that was sort of like theirs, plus a drawer full of workarounds.

What changed

AI got genuinely good at building things. Not “write a poem about my cat” good. Useful good. With the right person steering, it can turn a plain-English description like “when an order comes in, check stock, email the warehouse, and update the customer” into working software in days instead of months.

That changes the math. A tool that only twelve people will ever use used to be far too expensive to build. Now it often costs less than the hours those twelve people spend copying numbers from one tab to another.

The best software for your business might be the one nobody else would ever buy.

What that looks like in practice

  • An intake tool that reads incoming emails, pulls out the details, and files them where they belong, without anyone copying and pasting.
  • A dashboard that answers “where is that order?” so nobody has to go ask Dave. (Dave deserves a break.)
  • A quoting app that knows your pricing rules better than the spreadsheet does, and never turns a cell red out of spite.

The catch (there's always a catch)

AI is a brilliant, tireless, and occasionally overconfident intern. It writes code at superhuman speed and, now and then, confidently invents a feature nobody asked for. The magic isn't the AI on its own. It's knowing your process well enough to ask for the right thing, checking what comes back, and shaping it into something your team will actually use on a busy Tuesday afternoon.

That's the part I care about. Start with how your team really works, find the step that makes everyone sigh, and build the smallest tool that makes the sigh go away.

As for that spreadsheet: it's had a good run. It might be time to let it retire.
